Shortcut Jury

A free shortcut opens. Nobody disappears. Everyone knows the travel times and chooses for themselves. Will the average trip get better, worse, or stay the same?

The dotted road goes from A to B only. Road load counts all drivers using that segment, not just one route.

Can one driver escape? In each displayed allocation, no single driver can save time by switching routes, counting their own effect on congestion. That does not mean the group has its fastest possible arrangement.

Inspect the rules
  • This is a deliberately small mathematical game, not a forecast for real roads. Driver count stays identical in both comparisons. No traffic lights, crashes, induced demand, or hidden travel data.
  • The two congestion-sensitive roads each cost one minute per driver using them. The other two roads cost 45 minutes regardless of load. The shortcut costs zero minutes.
  • We exhaustively try every integer route allocation and keep the first where nobody can improve by moving alone. Tied alternatives are allowed. This finite-driver model is not the usual continuous-flow model; a stable allocation is not necessarily unique.
  • Route order below is upper (Start–A–Finish), lower (Start–B–Finish), shortcut (Start–A–B–Finish). All journeys go in the stated direction.
  • The paradox is not “new roads are always bad.” At a small crowd size this shortcut helps. At a large enough crowd size it is unused. Try both ends of the slider.

Inspired by Braess's paradox and questions about perfect information and disappearing traffic. The contribution here is an inspectable, fixed-population prediction experiment—not a new theorem.
